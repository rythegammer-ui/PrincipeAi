# Lesson 3: Choose What to Automate (and What Not To)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Course** | AI Automation Accelerator: Build Workflows & Agents |
| **Module** | 1 · Automation Foundations |
| **Length** | 5–7 min (~810 spoken words) |
| **Format** | Talking head + screen recording |

**Goal of this video:** Score candidate tasks, estimate the real time saved, choose a starting level of autonomy and recognize the tasks to leave with people.

## Before you record

- A spreadsheet or note open, ready to paste the time math template from the written lesson
- The scoring table and autonomy levels from the written lesson open for reference
- A short list of repetitive tasks you could use as examples while you talk (keep them generic)

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

The best first automation is rarely the most impressive one. It's the task that's frequent, tedious, low-risk and well understood. Pick well and you'll have a working win in days that builds trust for bigger projects.

Pick badly, and you'll spend weeks on something nobody uses. So in this lesson I'll give you a simple, repeatable way to choose.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Choose What to Automate (and What Not To)"]**

### 2. Score your candidates · 0:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Frequency · Time per run · Clarity · Cost of a mistake · Data access · Stability"]**

Start by listing every task you're tempted to automate. Then score each one from one to five on six criteria. Frequency: does it happen a few times a year, or many times a day?

Time per run: under a minute, or fifteen minutes or more? Clarity: is it "it depends" every time, or are there clear inputs, a clear output and past examples? Cost of a mistake: could an error cause legal, financial or reputational harm, or is it easy to spot and easy to undo?

Data access: is the information scattered, on paper or in someone's head, or is it already in apps that connect? And stability: does the process change monthly, or has it stayed the same for a year?

Add up the scores for a total out of thirty. As a rule of thumb, start with candidates scoring around twenty-two or more.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Cost of a mistake scores 1 or 2? A human approves every output."]**

And there's one special rule. If cost of a mistake scores a one or a two, a human must approve every output, no matter how high the total.

### 3. Do the time math · 1:40

**[ON CAMERA]**

Next, a quick estimate keeps you honest about value. I'll show you the formula from the written lesson.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this template:

~~~
Hours saved per month = runs per month x minutes saved per run / 60

Then subtract:
- review time    (minutes a human spends checking outputs)
- upkeep         (a little time each month for fixes and tuning)
- build time     (spread over the months you expect to use it)
~~~

Here's the illustrative example from the written lesson. If you handle a hundred and twenty enquiries a month and save six minutes on each, that's twelve hours a month before review and upkeep. But if a human still spends two minutes reviewing each one, the real saving is eight hours.

Still worthwhile, and now you know the true number. Don't forget the benefits that aren't time, either: faster responses, fewer dropped balls, consistent quality and work that happens outside office hours.

### 4. Choose the level of autonomy · 2:35

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "1 Assist · 2 Draft · 3 Act with review · 4 Autonomous"]**

Automation isn't all-or-nothing. At level one, Assist, the AI suggests when asked and the human does the work. At level two, Draft, the AI prepares outputs automatically and a human reviews, edits and sends.

At level three, Act with review, the AI handles routine cases and flags the rest, while a human checks flagged items and spot-checks others. At level four, Autonomous, the AI acts end to end and the human watches metrics and handles alerts.

**[ON CAMERA]**

Two rules make this work. First, start one level lower than you think you need. Customer-facing writing almost always starts at level two.

Second, promote with evidence, not optimism. Before moving up a level, decide what proof you need, like a target share of drafts sent with no or minor edits over several weeks. You'll learn to measure exactly that in Module 5.

### 5. What not to automate (yet) · 3:35

**[ON CAMERA]**

The most common mistake is automating something just because it's tempting. Some tasks are poor candidates. Decisions that need accountability, such as hiring, firing, credit, medical or legal judgments, stay with a person, though AI can prepare the information.

Rare tasks are better served by a checklist. Broken processes should be fixed first, because automating a mess gives you a faster mess. And relationship moments, like apologizing to a key client, delivering bad news or negotiating, belong to people.

Two more. If you can't tell whether the output is right, you can't trust it. And never send data you aren't allowed to share with an outside service under your contracts, policies or regulations.

**[OPTIONAL PERSONAL EXAMPLE: a task you once considered automating that turned out to be rare, risky or a broken process]**

### 6. A quick sanity check · 4:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Would anyone notice? · Who owns it? · 20 real examples? · What if the AI is wrong?"]**

Before you commit, answer four questions out loud. If this automation stopped tomorrow, would anyone notice within a week? Can you name the person who will own it?

Do you have at least twenty real past examples to test it with? And do you know what happens when the AI gets it wrong? Four yeses means go.

A "no" to the examples question is the most common blocker, and the easiest to fix. Start collecting examples now.

### 7. Build it · 5:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

For the Build it exercise in the written lesson, list ten tasks you or your team do repeatedly. Score each one on the six criteria and total them.

Pick your top candidate, check its cost-of-mistake score and choose a starting autonomy level. Run the time math with your own numbers, and write one sentence describing the evidence you'd need to move it up one level.

### 8. Recap and what's next · 5:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Score it · Do the math · Start one level lower · Promote with evidence"]**

Let's recap. Score candidates on frequency, time, clarity, cost of a mistake, data access and stability. Do the time math, including review and upkeep, so you know the real value.

Pick an autonomy level deliberately, start one level lower than you think and promote with evidence. And leave accountable decisions, rare tasks, broken processes and relationship moments to people.
