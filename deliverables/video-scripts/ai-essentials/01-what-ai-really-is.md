# Lesson 1: What AI Really Is (and Isn't)

| | |
|---|---|

| **Format** | Talking head + screen recording |

**Goal of this video:** Understand what an AI assistant actually is, what it's great at and where it falls short, using one simple mental model you can rely on for the rest of the course.

## Before you record

- Open any AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Copilot) in a new chat.
- Have both dinner prompts from the written lesson ready to paste: the one-line version and the detailed version. Send the detailed one in a new chat so the answers are easy to compare.
- Ask your editor to prepare the "great at" list and the "not" list as two simple title cards.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "What AI Really Is (and Isn't)"]**

If you've ever felt that everyone else "gets" AI and you somehow missed the memo, you're in good company. Here's the good news: you don't need to understand the math to use AI well.

You need one accurate mental model. By the end of this video, you'll have it.

### 2. What an AI assistant actually is · 0:20

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "LLM = large language model"]**

Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot: they're all built on something called a large language model, or LLM. It's been trained on an enormous amount of text: books, articles, websites, code and conversations. From all that reading, it learned the patterns of language well enough to write, explain, summarize and reason through problems in plain English.

Then it was trained further to be a helpful assistant. That's it. No magic, no consciousness, no little person inside.

But don't let "it's just patterns" fool you either. Those patterns capture a remarkable amount of human knowledge and skill.

### 3. A mental model that helps · 1:00

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "A brilliant new assistant on their first day"]**

Think of your AI assistant as a brilliant, extremely well-read new assistant on their first day. They've read more than any human ever could, they work in seconds, and they never mind redoing something ten times.

But they've never met you, so they don't know your job, family, preferences or goals unless you tell them. And sometimes they state things confidently that are simply wrong, without warning you.

Every good habit in this course flows from that picture. Because they've never met you, you'll learn to give context, and because they're fast and tireless, you'll learn to ask for changes. And because they can be confidently wrong, you'll learn when and how to check.

### 4. What it's great at, and what it's not · 1:50

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Drafting · Rewriting · Explaining · Organizing · Brainstorming · Summarizing"]**

So what is AI great at? Drafting, rewriting, explaining, organizing, brainstorming and summarizing. Notice the pattern: these are all language tasks.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Not a search engine · Not a database of facts · Not a mind reader · Not a professional · Not your memory"]**

And what is it not? It's not a search engine, unless your assistant searches the web for you, and many can. It's not a database of facts either, because it generates the most plausible response, which is usually right but not always.

It's not a mind reader, so vague requests get generic answers. It's not a professional: it can help you understand a medical, legal or financial topic, but it can't see your full situation or take responsibility for advice. And it's not your memory, because most assistants start each new conversation fresh.

### 5. See the difference · 2:40

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Give me dinner ideas.
~~~

Here's a typical first attempt. You get a reasonable list, but it's generic. It could be for anyone.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
I'm cooking for two adults and a 6-year-old who hates anything spicy.
We have about 30 minutes on weeknights and we're watching our budget.
Suggest 5 dinner ideas. No pasta, we already eat it a lot.
For each one, give a one-line description and the main ingredients.
~~~

Now look at the difference. Same assistant, a far better answer, because we treated it like a capable new assistant who needs a briefing. You'll master that skill in Module 2.

### 6. Common mistakes · 3:30

**[ON CAMERA]**

Three mistakes to avoid. First, treating it like a search box: three keywords get you three keywords' worth of help, so write the way you'd talk to a person.

Second, trusting it blindly, or dismissing it entirely. It's a powerful tool that needs a human in charge. And third, giving up after one answer, because the first reply is a starting point, not the finished product.

### 7. Try it now · 4:00

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Try it now"]**

Now it's your turn. Paste the "Try it now" prompt from the written lesson into any assistant. It asks the AI to explain what it is and isn't good at, then to ask you three questions about your daily life.

Answer them honestly, and notice how its suggestions improve once it knows a little about you. No assistant yet? Lesson 3 walks you through setup, so come back to this afterwards.

### 8. Recap and what's next · 4:30

**[ON CAMERA]**

Let's recap. AI assistants are large language models, trained on vast amounts of text. Picture a brilliant, tireless new assistant who has never met you and is sometimes confidently wrong.

They're strongest at language tasks. Better context in means better answers out, and important facts always deserve a check.

In the next lesson, How AI Assistants Work, and Why They Make Things Up, you'll see how an answer actually gets made, so you know when to trust it. See you there.
