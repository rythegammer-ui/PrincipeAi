# Lesson 2: How AI Assistants Work, and Why They Make Things Up

**Goal of this video:** Understand how an AI assistant writes its answers, why it sometimes states false things with confidence, and which kinds of answers you should double-check.

## Before you record

- Open your AI assistant in a new chat.
- Have both book prompts from the written lesson's worked example ready to paste: the book question and the confidence follow-up.
- Ask your editor to prepare the risk ladder from the written lesson (lower risk vs. higher risk) as a simple two-column graphic.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "How AI Assistants Work, and Why They Make Things Up"]**

Ask an AI assistant for a book recommendation, and it might suggest a title that sounds perfect, by a real author, that was never written. That isn't a glitch or a lie. It's a direct result of how these tools work.

Once you understand why it happens, you'll know when to trust an answer and when to double-check.

### 2. How an answer gets made · 0:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Most plausible, not verified"]**

When you send a message, the assistant doesn't look up a stored answer. It writes its reply one small piece at a time, called tokens, each time predicting what should come next. Those predictions are based on your message, the conversation so far and everything it learned in training.

Because it learned from so much human writing, its predictions are often excellent. But the core process is "produce the most plausible continuation," not "retrieve the verified truth." Usually those are the same thing, and sometimes they aren't.

### 3. Four things worth knowing · 1:00

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Cutoff date · Context window · No memory by default · Built to agree"]**

Four quick things worth knowing. One: training has a cutoff date, so ask about something newer and it may not know, or may guess. If timing matters, ask: "Did you search for this, or is it from your training?"

Two: it only sees what's in the current chat. That's its working memory, called the context window, and in a very long conversation early details can get missed. Three: it doesn't remember you between chats by default, unless your assistant has a memory feature.

And four: it's built to be agreeable. "Isn't this a great plan?" gets a different answer than "What are the weaknesses in this plan?"

### 4. Why AI "hallucinates" · 1:50

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Hallucination: something false, stated with confidence"]**

When AI states something false with complete confidence, that's called a hallucination. It happens because the assistant fills gaps with patterns, and the most plausible-sounding text can still be wrong. And a made-up answer sounds exactly as sure of itself as a correct one.

Errors cluster around specifics: names, dates, statistics, quotes, web links, page numbers and legal citations. Modern assistants hallucinate far less than early versions, but it still happens. So your job is to know where the risk is.

### 5. The risk ladder · 2:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Lower risk: drafting, explaining, summarizing your text · Higher risk: facts, numbers, sources, medical, legal, financial"]**

That's where the risk ladder comes in. Brainstorming, drafting, explaining well-established ideas and summarizing text you provided are lower risk, and AI shines there with little checking. Specific facts and numbers, recent events and prices, quotes and sources, and medical, legal and financial specifics are higher risk, so verify before you rely on them.

### 6. Catching a hallucination · 2:45

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
What are the best books about building better habits?
Include the author and year for each.
~~~

Here's the worked example from the written lesson. You get a tidy list, and most items are probably real. But one could be a plausible blend of a real author and a title they never wrote.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
For each book you listed, rate how confident you are that the title,
author and year are exactly right (high, medium or low). Flag anything
I should double-check before I buy it.
~~~

Self-ratings aren't perfect, but they reliably surface the shakiest items. Then a ten-second search for each flagged title settles it.

### 7. Common mistakes · 3:40

**[ON CAMERA]**

Three common mistakes. First, asking for sources and never opening them: AI can produce realistic-looking citations that don't exist, so click through.

Second, leading questions. Asking why something is true invites a defense of it, so ask whether it's true and what the evidence is on each side. And third, keeping one chat going forever: new topic, new chat.

### 8. Try it now · 4:05

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Try it now"]**

Your exercise: ask your assistant about something you know really well, like your hometown, your job or a hobby. Read the answer like an expert would. Is anything slightly off, outdated or invented?

Then paste the follow-up from the written lesson, which asks which parts of its answer are most likely to be wrong and how to verify them. Seeing AI's mistakes on a topic you know builds the instincts you'll need on topics you don't.

### 9. Recap and what's next · 4:40

**[ON CAMERA]**

Let's recap. AI predicts the most plausible next words; it doesn't retrieve verified facts. It has a training cutoff, a limited working memory per chat, and usually no memory of you between chats.

Hallucinations cluster around specifics, so use AI freely for drafting and thinking, and verify specifics before you rely on them.
