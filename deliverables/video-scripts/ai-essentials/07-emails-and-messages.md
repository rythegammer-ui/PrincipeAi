# Lesson 7: Emails, Texts & Tricky Messages

| | |
|---|---|
| **Course** | AI Essentials: Use AI in Everyday Life |
| **Module** | 3 · AI for Everyday Life |
| **Length** | 4–5 min (~620 spoken words) |
| **Format** | Talking head + screen recording |

**Goal of this video:** Use AI to turn your key points into clear messages, handle tricky and emotional replies calmly, and keep your messages sounding like you.

## Before you record

- Open your AI assistant in a new chat.
- Have the team-email notes prompt from the written lesson ready to paste, plus the "How is this likely to come across" follow-up from the "Try it now" exercise, to send in the same chat.
- Ask your editor to prepare the four everyday situations as a simple list graphic.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Emails, Texts & Tricky Messages"]**

How much of your week goes into writing messages? Not just emails, but texts to family, notes to your kid's school, a reply to a client, and that awkward message you've been avoiding for three days.

This is where AI pays off fastest for most people. Writing messages is pure language work, and you're the expert on whether the result sounds right.

### 2. The golden rule · 0:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "You bring the substance. AI handles the wording."]**

Here's the golden rule: you bring the substance, and AI handles the wording. AI is excellent at how to say something, but only you know what to say. So give it your key points, even in messy shorthand, and let it shape them.

### 3. From notes to email · 0:45

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Turn these notes into a clear, friendly email to my team:
- meeting moved from Tuesday to Thursday, same time
- bring your Q3 numbers
- Sam is presenting the new schedule
- lunch provided
Keep it under 100 words.
~~~

Here are four rough notes and a word limit. That's all it takes: thirty seconds of notes, a few seconds of AI, one clear email.

Before you send anything important, ask one more question.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
How is this likely to come across to the person reading it?
~~~

Think of this as a calm second pair of eyes. It's worth asking before you send anything important or emotional.

### 4. Four everyday situations · 1:40

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Replies · Saying no · Tricky messages · Complaints"]**

The written lesson has prompts for four everyday situations. First, replying when you don't know what to say: paste the message, then give your main point and the tone you want. Before you paste someone else's message, remove details they wouldn't want shared, like phone numbers or addresses.

Second, saying no without burning a bridge, with a short, kind reply that declines clearly and doesn't over-apologize. Third, the emotionally tricky message. When you're angry, hurt or anxious, paste what they said and your draft, and ask for a version that's honest about how you feel, but calm and constructive.

**[OPTIONAL PERSONAL EXAMPLE: a short moment when you were tempted to send an upset reply and a calmer second look would have helped]**

Fourth, complaints that get results. Give the facts and exactly what you want, and ask for firm, factual and polite. Clear facts plus a specific request are much harder to ignore than an emotional rant.

### 5. Keep your own voice · 2:35

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Give a sample · Ban the clichés · Do the final edit"]**

Messages that sound like a corporate brochure don't feel like you, and people notice. So give it a sample: paste two or three messages you've written and say, "Match this tone and style."

Ban the clichés, like "I hope this email finds you well." And do the final edit yourself, changing a word or two and adding a personal detail, because that last 10 percent makes it yours.

Here's how it comes together in the written lesson's example. You need to cancel on a friend's weekend get-together, so you jot down rough notes and ask for a warm, casual text that suggests a specific evening for dinner next week. You tweak one word and send it: two minutes instead of twenty minutes of overthinking.

### 6. Common mistakes · 3:30

The biggest mistake is sending without reading. Always check names, dates, numbers and promises, because AI can add details that sound right but aren't. And don't let AI decide what you say: you decide the message, and AI helps with the wording.

Also, a text to your sister shouldn't read like a press release, so ask for "casual." And strip out phone numbers, addresses and account details before you paste.

### 7. Try it now · 4:00

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Try it now"]**

Your turn. Find a message you've been putting off answering; there's always one. Write your key points in rough shorthand, draft a reply with the reply prompt in the written lesson, then ask how it's likely to come across.

Make it yours with a personal touch, check the details, and send it.

### 8. Recap and what's next · 4:20

**[ON CAMERA]**

Let's recap. Give AI your key points and let it handle the wording. Ask how it will come across before sending anything important or emotional.

Protect your voice with writing samples, banned clichés and a quick personal edit. And always check names, dates and commitments before you hit send.
