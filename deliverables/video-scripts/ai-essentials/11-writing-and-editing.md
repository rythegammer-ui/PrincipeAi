# Lesson 11: Write and Edit Better (Without Sounding Like a Robot)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Course** | AI Essentials: Use AI in Everyday Life |
| **Module** | 4 · AI for Work & Productivity |
| **Length** | 4–5 min (~610 spoken words) |
| **Format** | Talking head + screen recording |

**Goal of this video:** Use AI as an editor and writing partner, with specific instructions that make your writing clearer while keeping your own voice.

## Before you record

- Open your AI assistant in a new chat.
- Have a short draft ready to edit, one or two paragraphs long: an email, a post or a section of a report with no private details. A slightly wordy draft shows the edits best.
- In the first demo, replace the bracketed placeholder at the bottom of the prompt with your draft.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Write and Edit Better (Without Sounding Like a Robot)"]**

You've probably read text that was obviously written by AI. It's smooth, vague, oddly enthusiastic, and full of words like "delve" and "elevate." That's what happens when people hand AI the whole job.

In this lesson you'll learn the opposite approach: using AI as a sharp editor and writing partner. Your writing gets clearer and faster, and it still sounds like you.

### 2. Editor, not ghostwriter · 0:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Editor, not ghostwriter"]**

There are two ways to write with AI. The ghostwriter way is "Write a report about X." You get generic text that needs heavy rewriting and doesn't sound like you.

The editor way is different. You bring the ideas and a rough draft, and AI helps you structure, tighten and polish. It's faster overall and produces better writing, because the substance, the specifics and the voice all come from you.

Staring at a blank page? Don't ask for the finished piece. Ask for an outline built from your own thoughts, adjust it until it's right, then write each section yourself or draft it together one section at a time.

### 3. Demo: edit with specific instructions · 1:15

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Specific instructions get useful edits"]**

Once you have a draft, the key is being specific. "Improve this" gets random changes. Specific instructions get useful edits.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Edit the text below for clarity and flow. Keep my voice and all of my
key points. Don't add new ideas or facts. Then list the 5 most
important changes you made and why, so I can learn from them.

[PASTE YOUR DRAFT]
~~~

I swap the bracket at the bottom for a short draft. Now look at the answer: an edited version, then a list of the most important changes and the reasons for each.

That list is the secret. It makes you a better writer over time, and it makes it easy to reject edits you disagree with.

The written lesson has more focused edits that work well. For example, "Cut this by 30 percent without losing any key points," or "Find anything vague or unsupported, and ask me for the specifics."

### 4. Keep your voice · 2:15

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Show it your voice"]**

AI has a default style. To override it, show it yours. Paste two samples of your writing, ask it to describe your style in five bullet points, then have it edit your draft to match.

Save that style description, because you can paste it into future prompts or your personal instructions. It also helps to ban the giveaways, so as a follow-up, I'll add this:

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Avoid buzzwords and filler such as "delve," "elevate," "unlock,"
"in today's fast-paced world" and "it's important to note."
Use short, concrete sentences.
~~~

Now compare the new version with the first one, and look for shorter sentences and fewer empty phrases.

### 5. Mistakes to avoid · 3:05

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "You're the author"]**

A few mistakes to avoid. First, letting AI invent facts. Tell it, "Don't add facts I didn't give you," and then check anyway.

Second, accepting every edit. You're the author, so push back on changes that lose your meaning. And third, publishing the first draft, because AI drafts need a human pass for accuracy, voice and specifics.

One more thing: be honest about AI help. Some workplaces, schools and publications have specific rules about AI-assisted writing, so know what's expected. And when words are presented as personally yours, like a heartfelt letter, a personal statement or a reference, write the heart of it yourself.

### 6. Try it now · 3:50

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Try it now: edit, then list the changes"]**

Your turn. Take something you've written recently, like an email, a post or a section of a report, and run the "edit for clarity, then list your changes" prompt.

Accept the edits you agree with, reject the rest, and note one writing habit you learned. The prompt is in the written lesson.

### 7. Recap and what's next · 4:15

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "You bring the ideas. AI helps you polish."]**

To recap. Use AI as an editor and partner, not a ghostwriter, so the ideas and the draft come from you. And when you're stuck, start with an outline built from your own thoughts.

Give specific editing instructions, and ask AI to explain its changes. Then protect your voice with samples, a style description and a list of banned phrases.

In the next lesson, Research and Summarize Documents You Can Trust, you'll learn to get accurate summaries of long documents and pull out what matters, without falling for made-up sources. See you there.
