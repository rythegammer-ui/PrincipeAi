# Lesson 12: Research and Summarize Documents You Can Trust

| | |
|---|---|
| **Course** | AI Essentials: Use AI in Everyday Life |
| **Module** | 4 · AI for Work & Productivity |
| **Length** | 4–5 min (~600 spoken words) |
| **Format** | Talking head + screen recording |

**Goal of this video:** Get accurate, purpose-driven summaries of documents, back them with supporting quotes you can check, and research questions without falling for made-up sources.

## Before you record

- Open your AI assistant in a new chat.
- Pick a document that's fine to show on screen, with nothing confidential or personal in it: a public article, some terms and conditions or a school newsletter. Keep the original open so you can spot-check a quote.
- Decide how you'll fill the brackets in the first prompt: who the summary is for, what you need to know, and the document itself.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Research and Summarize Documents You Can Trust"]**

The 40-page report you're meant to read before Thursday. The meeting transcript nobody wants to go through. Three insurance quotes that use different words for the same thing.

Summarizing and comparing documents is one of AI's most practical superpowers. With the right technique, it's also one of the most reliable. In this lesson I'll show you that technique.

### 2. Why documents are a sweet spot · 0:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Most reliable: text you provide"]**

Remember the risk ladder from Module 1? AI is most reliable when it works from text you provide, and least reliable when it relies on memory.

When you paste or upload a document, the source is right in front of it. That doesn't make it perfect, but it makes it far more trustworthy, especially if you ask it to show its work.

### 3. Demo: a summary with a purpose · 0:50

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Give every summary a purpose"]**

"Summarize this" gets you a generic summary. A summary with a purpose gets you what you need. Here's the prompt from the written lesson.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Summarize the document below for [WHO, E.G. "ME, A BUSY PARENT"].
I need to know [YOUR PURPOSE, E.G. "WHAT CHANGES FOR OUR FAMILY AND
WHAT WE NEED TO DO"].

Give me:
1. The 3-5 key points, in plain English
2. Anything I need to do, with deadlines
3. Anything surprising, unusual or easy to miss

[PASTE OR ATTACH THE DOCUMENT]
~~~

I fill in who it's for and what I need to know, then add the document at the bottom. Look at the structure: the key points in plain English, anything I need to do with deadlines, and anything easy to miss.

### 4. Ask it to show its evidence · 1:35

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Ask for supporting quotes"]**

Now for the single best habit for trustworthy summaries. Add this to any summary prompt, or send it as a follow-up.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
For each key point, include a short direct quote from the document that
supports it. If something isn't in the document, say "not in the
document" rather than guessing.
~~~

Now every key point comes with a short quote. Search for that quote in the original, and you can check any point in seconds. If a quote isn't there, you've caught a problem.

The same idea works when you compare quotes, offers or policies. Ask it to mark anything missing as "not stated." That stops it from filling gaps with assumptions, and shows you exactly what to ask about.

The written lesson has that comparison prompt too, plus one that pulls the decisions, action items and open questions out of meeting notes.

### 5. Research, sources and limits · 2:40

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Open the sources"]**

When there's no document and you're asking AI to find information, the risk goes up. Three habits help. First, if your assistant can search the web, ask it to for anything recent or specific, and to include links.

Second, open the sources. Check that each link works and actually says what the AI claims, and prefer original sources, like official sites or the original study. Third, ask for disagreement: "What do critics or experts who disagree say?"

The most common mistake here is treating cited sources as verified. Open them, every time it matters.

And know the limits. Very long documents can exceed what your assistant reads at once, so if it seems to miss later sections, summarize in parts. Scans and photos may be read imperfectly, so spot-check the numbers.

Finally, don't upload confidential work documents, or files full of personal data, unless your assistant's settings and your organization's policies allow it. When in doubt, remove sensitive details first.

### 6. Try it now · 3:45

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Try it now: summarize, then spot-check"]**

Pick a real document, like some terms and conditions, a school newsletter or a work report you're allowed to share. Use the purpose-driven summary prompt, plus the supporting-quote instruction.

Then spot-check two of the quotes against the original. Did they match? Finally, ask, "What questions does this document leave unanswered?"

### 7. Recap and what's next · 4:10

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Purpose · Quotes · Not stated"]**

To recap. AI is most reliable when it works from text you provide, so give every summary a purpose and a structure. Ask for supporting quotes and "not stated" markers so you can verify quickly.

And for open-ended research, use web search, open the sources and look for disagreement. Every prompt from this video is in the written lesson.
