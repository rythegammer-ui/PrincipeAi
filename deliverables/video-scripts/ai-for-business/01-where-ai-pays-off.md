# Lesson 1: Where AI Actually Pays Off

| | |
|---|---|

**Goal of this video:** Viewers can name the four ways AI pays off in a business (time, quality, speed and revenue), know where it does and doesn't fit, and start listing the recurring tasks in their own business.

## Before you record

- Open your AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Copilot) in a new chat.
- Copy the prompt from the "Do this now" section of the written lesson. Decide in advance what goes in the brackets: your own business, or a clearly hypothetical one such as the six-person landscaping company from the written lesson.
- Keep the four payoffs table from the written lesson nearby for reference.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

Most business owners meet AI the same way. A few impressive demos, a handful of experiments, and then not much changes.

The tools aren't the problem. The problem is starting with the tool instead of the work.

This course flips that around. Before you write a single prompt, you'll learn to look at your business the way an implementation consultant does: as a set of workflows, some of which AI can make dramatically cheaper, faster or better.

### 2. The four payoffs · 0:35

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Time · Quality · Speed · Revenue"]**

Every worthwhile AI project pays off in at least one of four ways: time, quality, speed or revenue. If you can't name which one, you don't have a project yet. You have a demo.

Time is the easiest to see and measure. If a proposal takes you 90 minutes to write from scratch and 30 minutes to review and refine from a good AI draft, you've found an hour. Multiply that by how often it happens, and the value becomes obvious.

Quality is the payoff owners underrate. AI doesn't get tired at 6 p.m., so it can apply the same checklist to every quote and the same tone to every reply. That means fewer misunderstandings and less rework.

Speed is often worth more than the hours saved. A prospect who gets a thoughtful, specific reply within minutes is still in buying mode. One who hears back tomorrow may already be talking to someone else.

Revenue has the highest ceiling, and it's the hardest to attribute. It usually comes from work that wasn't happening at all: follow-ups nobody had time for, past customers nobody contacted, content nobody wrote.

### 3. Where AI fits, and where it doesn't · 1:55

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Drafting · Summarizing · Transforming · Classifying · Analyzing · Brainstorming"]**

Today's assistants, like Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot, excel at work made of language and patterns. They draft emails and proposals, summarize calls and long threads, and turn call notes into a proposal. They sort inquiries by type, explain data in plain English, and brainstorm offers and headlines.

Where doesn't AI pay off yet? Final decisions with real consequences, like pricing, hiring, legal positions and advice to clients. AI can prepare the decision, but a person who is accountable makes it.

It also struggles with facts it hasn't been given, where it can produce confident, plausible errors. Setting it up for something you do twice a year rarely pays back. And it can help you prepare for a difficult conversation, but it can't have it for you.

### 4. Live demo: find your opportunities · 2:50

Let's find some opportunities. This is the prompt from the "Do this now" section of the written lesson.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
I run a [TYPE OF BUSINESS] with [NUMBER] people. A typical week looks like this: [DESCRIBE BRIEFLY].

List 15 recurring tasks in a business like mine where an AI assistant could save time, improve quality, speed up responses or increase revenue. For each one, say which of those four payoffs it creates and what a person would still need to check. Only suggest things that work with off-the-shelf tools, not custom software.
~~~

I'll fill in the brackets with the type of business, the number of people, and a quick description of a typical week. Then watch what comes back.

Each task is tagged with a payoff and, just as important, with what a person would still need to check. Treat the list as raw material, not a verdict. Cross out what doesn't fit how you work, and add the tasks only you know about.

### 5. The rule: draft, don't decide · 3:40

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "AI drafts. Humans decide."]**

Here's the habit shared by the businesses that get lasting value from AI: AI drafts, humans decide. AI does the assembling, formatting and first-drafting. A person handles judgment, accuracy and anything a customer will see.

As evidence builds that a workflow is reliable, you can lighten the review on low-risk tasks. But you start with a human in the loop every time.

Picture a hypothetical six-person landscaping company. Quotes are drafted from site-visit notes, and the owner reviews, adjusts and sends them. Common email questions are answered from an approved FAQ and checked before sending.

And every quote triggers a scheduled follow-up, so fewer good jobs quietly go cold. None of this requires custom software. It requires a clear process, well-written prompts and a review step.

### 6. Do this now · 4:35

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Do this now: list 10 recurring tasks and their payoff"]**

Now it's your turn. Open a blank document and list ten recurring tasks you or your team did this week. Next to each one, write which payoff improving it would create: time, quality, speed or revenue.

If you get stuck, use the prompt I just ran; it's in the written lesson. And keep your list, because it's the raw material for the next lesson.

### 7. Recap and what's next · 5:05

**[ON CAMERA]**

So, start with the work, not the tool. Every AI project should create time, quality, speed or revenue. AI is strongest at language and pattern work, while final decisions, fact-checking and relationships stay with people.

In the next lesson, Map Your Workflows in One Afternoon, you'll turn that list into a full inventory of the recurring work in your business. See you there.
