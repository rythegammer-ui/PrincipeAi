# Lesson 2: Map Your Workflows in One Afternoon

| | |
|---|---|
| **Course** | AI for Business Owners: The Implementation Playbook |
| **Module** | 1 · The AI Opportunity Audit |
| **Length** | 5–6 min (~730 spoken words) |
| **Format** | Talking head + screen recording |

**Goal of this video:** Viewers can build a task inventory of the recurring work in their business, gather input from their team, and flag which tasks show the signals of a strong AI candidate.

## Before you record

- Open your AI assistant in a new chat.
- Prepare a page of rough notes, or a short voice-memo transcript, describing a typical week in a business. Use your own business or a clearly hypothetical one, such as the 15-person home cleaning company from the written lesson.
- Copy the "organize your notes" prompt from the written lesson, ready to paste.
- Have a blank spreadsheet open to show the inventory columns.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

You can't improve what you can't see. Most owners carry their workflows around in their heads. That's why AI efforts tend to target whatever is most visible or most annoying, rather than what's most valuable.

In this lesson, you'll build a task inventory: a simple table of the recurring work in your business. It takes an afternoon, and it's the foundation for every decision in the rest of the course.

### 2. Think in workflows, not tools · 0:30

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Trigger → Steps → Output"]**

Start by describing work as a workflow with three parts: a trigger, a series of steps, and an output.

Say a new inquiry arrives; that's the trigger. Someone reads it, works out what the person needs, replies and logs it in the CRM; those are the steps. The output is a booked call or a polite no.

When you describe work this way, AI opportunities tend to jump out. They're usually the steps that involve reading, writing, summarizing, sorting or remembering to do something.

### 3. Build the inventory · 1:05

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Marketing · Sales · Delivery · Customer service · Admin and finance · People · Leadership"]**

Go area by area, so nothing hides: marketing, sales, delivery, customer service, admin and finance, people, and leadership. Then open a spreadsheet and give every recurring task its own row.

The written lesson lists all the columns. The core ones are the task, written verb-first, like "Write quote after site visit," the trigger, how often it happens per month, and the minutes each time takes.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Hours per month = frequency × minutes ÷ 60"]**

Multiply frequency by minutes and divide by 60, and you have hours per month. Add who does it, the inputs, the output, and a pain score from one to five for how much people dislike or delay it.

In the written lesson's hypothetical home cleaning company, the office manager replies to about 120 booking inquiries a month, at six minutes each. That's 12 hours a month on a single task.

Don't chase precision. Estimates are fine here, because you'll measure properly for the projects you choose.

### 4. Interview your team · 2:10

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "What do you dread, postpone or rush?"]**

The people doing the work know where the friction is. Spend 15 minutes with each person, or each role. Ask what feels repetitive, what they dread, postpone or rush, where they wait on someone else, and what customers ask most often.

And one more: if you had three extra hours a week, what would you do with them?

Here's the mistake to avoid. If people think this is about replacing them, fear produces polite, useless answers.

So say clearly at the start that this is about removing drudgery, not replacing people. Honesty produces your best opportunities.

**[OPTIONAL PERSONAL EXAMPLE: one sentence about a time a team member's honest answer showed you a task you hadn't realized was eating their week.]**

### 5. Demo: let AI organize your notes · 2:50

Your first pass will probably be messy, and that's fine. A helpful trick is to record yourself talking through a typical week, get a transcript, and let AI structure it.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Below are my rough notes about the recurring work in my business. Turn them into a table with these columns: Task, Area, Trigger, Frequency per month, Minutes each, Who does it, Inputs, Output.

Rules:
- Don't invent numbers. If frequency or time is missing, write "?" and list the questions I need to answer at the end.
- Split vague items into separate tasks ("admin" is not a task).
- Start every task with a verb.
- Group the table by area.

My notes:
"""
[PASTE NOTES OR VOICE-MEMO TRANSCRIPT]
"""
~~~

Look at the rules. The AI isn't allowed to invent numbers, so anything missing comes back as a question mark, with a list of questions for you at the end.

Vague items like "admin" get split into real tasks, each starting with a verb. The written lesson also has a follow-up prompt that asks which tasks businesses like yours usually have that are missing from your list. It asks you about each one rather than adding it, so the inventory stays grounded in how you actually work.

### 6. Flag the AI-friendly signals · 3:55

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Language-heavy · Repetitive · Good examples · Digital inputs · Quick to check"]**

Now tick the AI-friendly signals for each task. More ticks means a stronger candidate.

Is it language-heavy and repetitive? Do you have good examples of what "done well" looks like, and are the inputs already digital? Can a person check the output much faster than creating it?

Watch for red flags, too: tasks that need physical presence, carry legal or financial consequences, depend on knowledge nobody has written down, or happen too rarely to be worth the setup.

### 7. Do this now · 4:30

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Do this now: 25–40 tasks, sorted by hours"]**

Your exercise is to build your inventory. Aim for 25 to 40 tasks across all areas, fill in the hours per month, and sort from most hours to fewest. Then tick the signals for every task.

Sorting by hours puts your biggest time sinks at the top, and the ticks show which of them are strong AI candidates. The full column list and both prompts are in the written lesson.

### 8. Recap and what's next · 5:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

Describe work as trigger, steps and output, because AI opportunities usually sit in the steps. Inventory every area, so the most valuable work doesn't hide behind the most visible. And ask your team, and make it safe to answer honestly.

In the next lesson, Score Your Opportunities: Impact × Ease × Risk, you'll score these tasks and turn them into a ranked list.
