# Lesson 3: Score Your Opportunities: Impact × Ease × Risk

| | |
|---|---|
| **Course** | AI for Business Owners: The Implementation Playbook |
| **Module** | 1 · The AI Opportunity Audit |
| **Length** | 5–6 min (~710 spoken words) |
| **Format** | Talking head + screen recording |

**Goal of this video:** Viewers can score every task in their inventory on impact, ease and risk, calculate a priority score, and sanity-check the ranking before choosing projects.

## Before you record

- Open your AI assistant in a new chat.
- Prepare a short scored table to paste, such as the hypothetical home renovation company's table from the written lesson, or a few rows from your own inventory.
- Copy the "Let AI challenge your scores" prompt from the written lesson, ready to paste.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

Your inventory probably has 30 or more tasks, and you can't tackle them all. The most common mistake at this stage is starting with the most exciting idea rather than the most valuable one.

A simple scoring model keeps you honest. It gives you a ranked list you can defend to your team, your business partner or yourself.

### 2. The three scores · 0:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Impact · Ease · Risk, each scored 1 to 5"]**

Score each task from one to five on three dimensions. The written lesson has a rubric for each, so everyone scores the same way, and the ranking holds up when someone asks why.

Impact asks how much value you get if it works, across all four payoffs. A one is a minor convenience that saves under an hour a month. A five saves 20 or more hours a month, or materially changes revenue, capacity or customer experience.

Ease asks how easy it is with off-the-shelf tools. A five works with a good prompt in an assistant you already use. A one needs custom development, or the knowledge isn't written down anywhere.

Risk asks what happens if it goes wrong, and here a higher number means more risk. A one is internal, easy to check and involves no sensitive data. A five carries legal, financial, health, safety or employment consequences.

### 3. The formula · 1:30

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Priority = Impact × Ease × (6 − Risk)"]**

Then multiply: impact, times ease, times six minus risk. Subtracting risk from six flips it, so a safer task scores higher.

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "60+ first projects · 30–59 later · under 30 park it"]**

Scores run from 1 to 125, and the written lesson gives a rough guide. Sixty and above is a strong candidate for your first projects. Thirty to 59 is worth doing later, and below 30, park it for now.

High-risk tasks score low by design. That doesn't mean never. It means not first, and not without strong guardrails and, where relevant, professional advice.

### 4. What the scores reveal · 2:05

In the written lesson's hypothetical renovation company, following up after sending a quote wins with a score of 80, even though it takes only six hours a month. It touches revenue directly, it's easy to set up, and it's low risk. Often the biggest opportunity is work that isn't happening.

Drafting quotes from site-visit notes has the most hours, but it scores lower because it needs a usable price list and scope templates first. That makes it a perfect second project once those exist.

And screening job applicants ranks last, because employment decisions carry legal and fairness risk. AI can still help write the job ad and interview guide, without judging candidates.

### 5. Demo: let AI challenge your scores · 2:55

You know your business better than any AI, but a sparring partner helps you spot blind spots.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Act as a skeptical operations consultant. Below is my AI opportunity list with Impact, Ease and Risk scores from 1 to 5 (for Risk, 5 = highest risk). Priority = Impact x Ease x (6 - Risk).

For each task:
1. Say whether any score looks too optimistic or too pessimistic, and why.
2. Name the biggest risk I may have missed.
3. Suggest one practical way to lower the risk or raise the ease.

Then list my top five tasks in priority order. Be direct and don't flatter my choices.

My list:
"""
[PASTE YOUR SCORED TABLE]
"""
~~~

I'll paste a scored table into the triple quotes. For each task, the AI flags scores that look off, names a risk you may have missed, and suggests a way to lower the risk or raise the ease.

Treat the response as input, not instruction. If it raises a fair point, change the score. If it doesn't understand your business, ignore it.

### 6. Adjust for reality · 3:40

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Gut check · Dependencies · Reduce risk, then re-score"]**

Scores are a starting point, not a verdict, so run three checks. First, a gut check: if a ranking feels wrong, look for a scoring error or a hidden factor, like a task being someone's favorite part of their job.

Second, dependencies. A brand voice guide improves every marketing task, and a written price list makes quote drafting possible, so give enabling projects a bump.

Third, reduce risk to raise the score. Remove personal data, keep it internal at first, or add a review step, then score again. Many 30s become 60s this way.

### 7. Do this now · 4:20

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Do this now: score, sort, highlight your top eight"]**

Add Impact, Ease, Risk and Priority columns to your inventory. Score every task that takes two or more hours a month, or your top 20 by hours, then calculate the priority and sort from highest to lowest.

Then highlight your top eight. That's the shortlist you'll choose from next, so don't agonize over small differences; scoring is a tool for thinking, not a verdict. The rubrics and the prompt are in the written lesson.

### 8. Recap and what's next · 4:55

**[ON CAMERA]**

Score every opportunity on impact, ease and risk with shared rubrics, then rank by impact times ease times six minus risk. The best first project is often work that isn't happening at all. And high-risk tasks aren't banned; they're sequenced later, with guardrails.

In the next lesson, Choose Your First Three Projects, you'll pick your starting three from that shortlist.
