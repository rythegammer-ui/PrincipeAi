## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

The difference between "AI is a toy" and "AI saves me five hours a week" is almost always the brief.

Treat a prompt the way you'd delegate to a capable new hire. They're smart and fast, but they know nothing about your business, your customers or your standards unless you tell them. Vague delegation gets vague work, from people and from AI.

In this lesson, you'll learn the six-part business brief, and you'll see the difference it makes, live.

### 2. The six-part business brief · 0:35

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Goal · Context · Inputs · Constraints · Format · Examples"]**

Strong business prompts cover six things. The goal is what the output is for and what success looks like. Context is who you are, who the audience is, and any background that changes the answer.

Inputs are the material to work from, like notes or a transcript, kept clearly separate from your instructions. Constraints cover length, tone, what to include or avoid, and which facts must not be invented. Format is exactly what to return.

And examples are one or two samples of "good." That's the single most powerful thing you can add. You won't need all six every time, but the more the task matters, the more of them you should include.

### 3. Demo: before and after · 1:20

Let me show you the difference. Here's a typical first attempt.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Write a follow-up email to a prospect.
~~~

You get something polite, generic and unusable. It has no goal, no context, nothing to work from, and no idea what good looks like.

Now here's the same request as a business brief, with the brackets already filled in.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
GOAL: Draft a follow-up email to a prospect after a discovery call. Success = they book a 30-minute proposal review.

CONTEXT: We are [COMPANY], a [WHAT YOU DO] for [WHO YOU SERVE]. The prospect is [NAME, ROLE, COMPANY]. They're comparing us with one other provider.

INPUTS (my call notes):
"""
[PASTE NOTES]
"""

CONSTRAINTS:
- Under 150 words. Warm, direct, no hype.
- Reference two specific things they told us.
- Use only facts from my notes. If something is missing, write [CHECK] instead of guessing.
- One clear call to action using this link: [BOOKING LINK]

FORMAT: Subject line, then the email, then one alternative subject line.

EXAMPLE OF OUR TONE:
"""
[PASTE A PAST EMAIL YOU LIKED]
"""
~~~

Each part is labeled, and the notes and the example sit inside triple quotes, so the AI can tell my instructions apart from the content it's working on.

Look at the constraints: under 150 words, two specific things they told us, only facts from my notes, and one clear call to action. That's what turns a generic email into one that sounds like it came from someone who listened.

It's longer to write the first time. But it becomes a reusable template, and the output is something you can send after a quick review.

### 4. The CHECK rule · 2:55

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Missing facts? Mark them CHECK. Don't guess."]**

Here's the habit that matters most for anything factual. AI fills gaps with plausible guesses, and in business, a plausible guess about a price, a date or a promise is a liability.

So build one line into every prompt that touches facts: use only the information I've provided, and if something is missing or unclear, write CHECK in square brackets instead of guessing.

Then search the draft for CHECK before anything goes out. It's a tiny habit that makes invented details far easier to catch. You're not asking for a perfect first draft; you're asking for one where every gap is visible.

### 5. Four techniques the pros use · 3:40

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Ask questions first · Work in steps · Ask for options · Self-critique"]**

Four techniques make any brief stronger. First, for anything complex, ask it to ask you up to five questions before it starts. The questions often reveal what you forgot to say.

Second, break big jobs into steps: approve an outline, then draft section by section. Third, ask for options, like three subject lines in different styles, which turns you from writer into editor.

Fourth, make it critique itself. Ask it to check its draft against your constraints, list anything it misses, then fix it. Or ask what a skeptical customer would object to.

### 6. Do this now · 4:20

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Do this now: one task, a full brief, run it twice"]**

Take one task from your first three projects and write a full six-part brief for it. Run it, then run it again with one real example of "good" added, and compare the two results side by side. Save the better prompt.

The written lesson also has three templates worth saving today: one for turning messy notes into a clean document, one for replying to a customer message, and one for getting a blunt second opinion.

The customer reply template has a smart safeguard. If your policies don't cover something, the AI puts a note to you above the draft instead of making up an answer.

### 7. Recap and what's next · 5:05

**[ON CAMERA]**

Prompt like you delegate: goal, context, inputs, constraints, format and examples. Examples of "good" are the most powerful thing you can add. And use the CHECK rule, so missing facts get flagged instead of invented.
