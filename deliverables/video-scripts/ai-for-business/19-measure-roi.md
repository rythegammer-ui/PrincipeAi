## Before you record

- Your AI assistant open in a new chat.
- A spreadsheet with the ROI calculator from the written lesson set up, filled in with the lesson's hypothetical quote-drafting example. Have it ready to show on screen.
- A short set of fictional before-and-after notes for one pilot to paste into the review prompt, plus a made-up loaded hourly cost, tool cost and setup hours to fill in its brackets.

## Script

### 1. Hook · 0:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Lesson 19 · Measure ROI: Baseline, Measure, Iterate"]**

"We use AI and it feels faster" isn't ROI. Measuring tells you where to invest more, what to fix or stop, and whether your tool spend is justified.

And it doesn't require complicated analytics. It requires a baseline, a consistent way to measure and a regular review.

### 2. The three-step loop · 0:20

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Baseline · Measure · Iterate"]**

Here's the loop. First, baseline: measure the current process before you change it. Second, measure: after the change, measure the same way over a similar period.

Third, iterate: improve, scale or stop, based on what you find. Without the first step, you can't prove anything.

### 3. Set the baseline · 0:40

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Time · Quality · Speed · Revenue"]**

For each pilot, choose one to three metrics that match its payoff. Time might be minutes per task. Quality might be edits needed, errors or complaints.

Speed might be time to first reply or turnaround time. And revenue might be your follow-up completion rate or quote-to-win rate.

Keep measurement simple. Time five to ten instances of the task with a stopwatch or time tracker, pull response times from your inbox or CRM, or count volumes for two weeks. Rough but consistent beats precise but never done.

### 4. Build the ROI calculator · 1:15

**[CUT TO SCREEN: the ROI calculator spreadsheet from the written lesson, filled in with its hypothetical example]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Hypothetical example"]**

The written lesson gives you a calculator you can set up in any spreadsheet. It runs from tasks per month and minutes per task, before and after, through hours saved, loaded hourly cost, tool costs and setup cost, to net monthly benefit and payback period.

The example in it is hypothetical, for a business that writes 20 quotes a month.

Each quote took 75 minutes before, and 30 minutes after, including review. That's 15 hours saved a month. At a loaded cost of $45 an hour, that time is worth $675 a month.

Subtract $60 a month in tools, and the net benefit is $615 a month. The one-off setup, 10 hours at $45, is $450, so it pays back in under a month.

### 5. Three mistakes to avoid · 2:10

**[ON CAMERA]**

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Include review time · Use loaded cost · Don't claim every revenue change"]**

People often get three things wrong. First, they leave review time out of the "after" number. Measure the whole new process, not just how fast the AI generates a draft.

Second, they use hourly pay instead of loaded cost. Include taxes, benefits and overhead, and for the owner's time, use what an hour is worth on revenue-generating work.

Third, they claim every revenue change. Compare like with like and consider other explanations, like seasonality, price changes or a new marketing push. Count it when the comparison is reasonable, and say how confident you are.

### 6. Use the time, and watch quality · 2:50

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "'15 hours reinvested in follow-up calls' beats '15 hours saved'"]**

Saved hours easily disappear into other busywork. So decide in advance where they'll go, such as more sales calls, faster delivery, better service or simply fewer late nights, and track it. "Fifteen hours a month reinvested in follow-up calls" is a far stronger result than "fifteen hours saved."

Speed can also hide problems. Alongside time, track errors caught in review, corrections after sending and customer complaints. If errors rise, slow down and improve the prompt, the examples or the SOP before scaling.

### 7. Iterate with a 30-day review · 3:25

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Clear win: standardize · Promising: improve · Little gain: refocus or stop · Quality problems: tighten or stop"]**

At 30 days, every pilot gets a decision. A clear win gets standardized. Promising but inconsistent means improving prompts, examples and inputs, then re-measuring in two weeks.

Little or no gain means checking whether the real bottleneck is somewhere else, then refocusing or stopping. Quality problems mean tightening review, narrowing the scope, or stopping.

And stopping a project that isn't working is a success of the process, not a failure. It frees time for the next opportunity on your list.

AI can help you run the numbers for the review.

**[SCREEN RECORDING]** Paste this prompt:

~~~
Here's the before-and-after data for an AI pilot in my business:
"""
[PASTE METRICS AND NOTES]
"""
1. Calculate hours saved, monthly value and payback period, using a loaded hourly cost of [$X], tool costs of [$Y] per month and [Z] setup hours.
2. Show each step of the calculation so I can check it.
3. Point out anything that could make this result misleading, such as a small sample, seasonality or a learning curve.
4. Recommend whether to standardize, improve or stop, and explain why.
~~~

The prompt asks it to show each step of the calculation, so check the arithmetic yourself, using the verification habits from Module 5. And pay attention to what it says could make the result misleading.

### 8. Do this now · 4:35

**[ON SCREEN TEXT: "Build the calculator · Fill in your baselines · Book the 30-day review"]**

Here's your exercise. Build the calculator for your three pilot projects and fill in the baselines. If you haven't measured them yet, do it this week, before the new process starts.

Then confirm the 30-day review in your calendar. The written lesson has a one-table scorecard to review with your team each month: project, owner, metric, baseline, now, net monthly value and the decision.

### 9. Recap and what's next · 5:00

**[ON CAMERA]**

Let's recap. Baseline, measure and iterate; without a baseline you can't prove anything. Include review time, use loaded hourly cost and be careful attributing revenue.

Decide where saved time will be reinvested, and track quality alongside speed. Standardize the winners, fix the promising ones and stop the rest without regret.
